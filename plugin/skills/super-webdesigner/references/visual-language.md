# Visual language: what the page is made of

The brief gives the palette (question 1) and the feeling (question 2). But a
page is also made of edges, depth, lines, surfaces, type and movement, and an
agent left alone picks those from habit: the same radius and shadow on every
block, or, when told to avoid that, hard shadows and black outlines on every
block. Both ignore the business.

Decide the six materials below for each direction, from the same real-world
object and feeling. Write one reason per line. A material without a reason is
the agent's default.

## The six materials

| Material | Decide | Ask |
|---|---|---|
| **Edges** | Corner radius: none, small, soft, round, organic (cut-outs, irregular shapes) | What shape do the real objects of this business have? Bread, ceramics and cushions are round; steel, paper and glass are sharp |
| **Depth** | Flat, soft natural shadow, layered paper, real photo depth | Where does light really fall in the reference? Shadow only where there is light to cast it |
| **Lines** | No borders, hairlines, thick rules, drawn lines | Does anything in the reference have outlines? Most real things don't |
| **Surface** | Flat color, photo, paper, flour, linen, concrete, metal | What would the visitor touch in the real place? |
| **Type voice** | Warm, sharp, hand-made, official, playful, quiet | How would the owner talk to a regular customer? |
| **Motion feel** | Slow and soft, quick and crisp, springy, mechanical | How do things move in the real place: dough rising, a knife on a board, a machine stamping? |

Separation by role, not by habit: border, fill, radius and shadow each mark an
element as a separate object. Give them to the one element that has to stand
apart (the main button, the price, the product photo), not to every block. The
same radius and shadow on everything flattens the page.

## Starting points by feeling

Not rules: the reference wins. Use them to catch a choice that fights the
feeling.

| Feeling | Usually fits | Usually fights it |
|---|---|---|
| Warm, hand-made, homey (bakery, café, family restaurant) | Soft or organic edges, natural photo light, paper or flour texture, a humanist or rounded face, slow easing | Hard offset shadows, black outlines, mono labels, technical data, mechanical snaps |
| Cared for, calm (clinic, spa, therapist) | Soft edges, lots of space, light surfaces, gentle fades | Dense grids, sharp contrast everywhere, fast motion |
| Trust, seriousness (law, accounting, bank) | Restrained edges, few lines, one strong photo, steady motion | Playful bounces, emoji, loud colors |
| Energy, appetite (burger, bar, gym) | Big type, strong contrast, tight crops, quick motion | Pale pastels, long fades, too much empty space |
| Precise, technical (engineering, tools, dev products) | Sharp edges, hairlines, grotesk or mono for real data, crisp motion | Script fonts, soft blurry shadows |

A direction can break this table on purpose, when the brief's collision asks
for it. Then say so in the direction ("B puts a cold, sharp frame around warm
photos to feel like a gallery"), and check that the person chose that
contrast, not you.

## The fit test

Before showing a direction, and again on the finished page:

1. Read the six materials next to the feeling. Does each one push toward it?
2. Look at the main photo and the frame around it. Do they tell the same
   story? A soft, warm loaf inside a hard box with an offset shadow tells two.
3. Any material that fights the feeling without a reason the person approved:
   change it before going on.

## In the direction

Each skeleton lists its six materials in one line each, and
`design/direction.md` keeps them (see `direction-template.md`). The build
follows them: they're as binding as the palette.
